What Race Is,and Isn't

Most of us grew up treating race as something obvious, something you can see. This page tests that idea three ways: with genetics, with history, and with sociology. Work through it in order. Try every activity before you read the answer.

Science

Is race biological?

If races were natural biological groups, we would expect three things. You could draw clear lines between them. People inside a race would be genetically alike. And the trait we use to sort people, skin color, would tell us about other traits too. Let's test each one.

From the reading

"If you juxtapose people from the Congo in West Africa, and people from Norway, you might conclude that the two peoples looked so different that they were of different 'races.' However, if you walked all the way from the Congo through Egypt and the Middle East to Norway, you would find that skin color varies very gradually—almost imperceptibly—as you travel. There is no place where one color of skin gives way abruptly to another… Famously, Livingstone (1962), a population biologist, concluded, 'there are no races, there are only clines.'"

Why the gradient? Skin color tracks sunlight. People need enough ultraviolet light to make vitamin D, but too much destroys folate, which healthy reproduction depends on. Near the equator, darker skin protected people. Far north, lighter skin let in enough light. Autumn UV levels alone explain about 86% of the variation in human skin color (Jablonski & Chaplin, 2013).

Kay Young McChesney, "Teaching Diversity: The Science You Need to Know to Explain Why Race Is Not Biological," SAGE Open (2015). Excerpted under a CC-BY 3.0 license.

Ancestry: how doctors describe it now

Ancestry is real biology. Race is a set of boxes people drew on top of it, with edges that nature doesn't have.

"But my ancestry test gave me percentages."

Ancestry is real. Human populations roughly cluster by geography, and with thousands of genetic markers combined, a test can estimate where your ancestors lived. That's ancestry: a history of places, with blurry edges. A race is a box society puts you in. Scientists now prefer "ancestry" for exactly this reason. Your percentages also shift when a company updates its reference groups.

History

Where did race come from?

From the reading

"During the 18th century, the idea that humans could be divided into different biological races that were correlated with different traits and characteristics, such as 'intelligent and hardworking' versus 'unintelligent and indolent,' seems to have appeared about the same time that chattel slavery was rapidly expanding as a money-making opportunity for Europeans. This suggests that the idea of race had a social genesis tied to the justification of slavery."

McChesney (2015), citing Smedley & Smedley (2005). CC-BY 3.0.

The first "races," 1758

Read the labels

Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus divided humans into four types by continent and skin color. Then he added personalities. These are his labels, not facts. Notice how the stereotypes were built in from the very start.

His label"Yellow"

His label"Black"
Assigned traits"Lazy, slow, relaxed"

Linnaeus said the traits came from an excess of one of the four "bodily humors," a medical theory that was already outdated. The U.S. still uses close versions of his four categories. Some of the stereotypes are still around, too.

Sociology

Is race real?

Parts 1 and 2 might make it sound like race is fake. Sociologists would say that's the wrong conclusion. Race isn't real the way blood type is real. It's real the way money or a border is real: people made it up, and it still shapes everyone's life.

"If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences."W. I. Thomas & Dorothy Swaine Thomas, 1928 · the Thomas theorem

From the reading

"Race is not something rooted in nature, something that reflects clear and discrete variations in human identity. But race is also not an illusion. While it may not be 'real' in a biological sense, race is indeed real as a social category with definite social consequences."

Omi & Winant, Racial Formation in the United States (2015), quoted in McChesney (2015).

Quick Read #2

Who gets pain medicine

White patients in U.S. emergency rooms are more likely than Black patients to be given opioids for the same kinds of pain. Nothing about biology explains it. Doctors' assumptions do.

Quick Read #4

Who gets the callback

Identical résumés got fewer calls when the name signaled the applicant was Black. Same skills on paper, different result.

Part 2, again

Who gets counted

Census race data decides how voting districts are drawn, how civil rights laws are enforced and where federal money goes. The boxes are invented, and the money is real.
