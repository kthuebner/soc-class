Science

Is race biological?

Sociologists define a race as a socially constructed category of people who share biologically transmitted traits that members of a society consider important. Many people skip the "socially constructed" part and assume race is pure biology. If races were natural biological groups, we would expect three things. You could draw clear lines between them. People inside a race would be genetically alike. And the trait we use to sort people, skin color, would tell us about other traits too. Let's test each one.

From the reading

"If you juxtapose people from the Congo [in Central Africa], and people from Norway, you might conclude that the two peoples looked so different that they were of different 'races.' However, if you walked all the way from the Congo through Egypt and the Middle East to Norway, you would find that skin color varies very gradually—almost imperceptibly—as you travel. There is no place where one color of skin gives way abruptly to another… Famously, Livingstone (1962), a population biologist, concluded, 'there are no races, there are only clines.'"

Why the gradient? Skin color tracks sunlight, and the pattern is natural selection at work. People need enough ultraviolet light to make vitamin D, but too much destroys folate, which healthy reproduction depends on. Near the equator, darker skin protected people. Far north, lighter skin let in enough light. UV radiation explains a large share of the global variation in human skin color (Jablonski & Chaplin, 2013).

Kay Young McChesney, "Teaching Diversity: The Science You Need to Know to Explain Why Race Is Not Biological," SAGE Open (2015). Excerpted under a CC-BY 3.0 license.

Ancestry: how doctors describe it now

Ancestry is real biology. Race is a set of boxes people drew on top of it, with edges that nature doesn't have.

"But my ancestry test gave me percentages."

Ancestry is real. Human populations roughly cluster by geography, and with thousands of genetic markers combined, a test can estimate where your ancestors lived. That's ancestry: a history of places, with blurry edges. A race is a box society puts you in. Scientists now prefer "ancestry" for exactly this reason. Your percentages also shift when a company updates its reference groups.

"Wait. If race isn't biological, why do doctors ask about it?"

So should doctors ignore race? No. Race can sometimes be useful because it can hint at a patient's ancestry, environment, experiences or exposure to discrimination. But race is usually a rough proxy. When doctors know the actual risk factor, such as a specific gene, a patient's ancestry or something in their environment, that is usually more informative. Sickle cell is the example: the gene matters, not the race box.

History

Where did race come from?

From the reading

"During the 18th century, the idea that humans could be divided into different biological races that were correlated with different traits and characteristics, such as 'intelligent and hardworking' versus 'unintelligent and indolent,' seems to have appeared about the same time that chattel slavery was rapidly expanding as a money-making opportunity for Europeans. This suggests that the idea of race had a social genesis tied to the justification of slavery."

McChesney (2015), citing Smedley & Smedley (2005). CC-BY 3.0.

The first "races," 1758

Read the labels

Linnaeus didn't discover four human races. He created a system for classifying humans.

He was trying to classify humans the way he classified plants and animals, so he divided them into four types by continent and skin color. Then he added personalities. His categories reflected his era's ideas about geography, behavior and human difference. These are his labels, not facts. Notice how the stereotypes were built in from the very start.

Linnaeus said the traits came from an excess of one of the four "bodily humors," a medical theory that was already outdated. The U.S. still uses close versions of his four categories. Some of the stereotypes are still around, too. Ranking groups as naturally better or worse than one another became one of the foundations of racism.

3

Sociology

Is race real?

Parts 1 and 2 might make it sound like race is fake. Sociologists would say that's the wrong conclusion. Race is a product of the social construction of reality: people created the categories through interaction, law and custom, then treated them as natural. Race isn't real the way blood type is real. It's real the way money or a border is real: people made it up, and it still shapes everyone's life.

So the real question isn't "Is race real?" It's this: how can something people created become real enough to change people's lives?

"If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences."W. I. Thomas & Dorothy Swaine Thomas, 1928 · the Thomas theorem

From the reading

"Race is not something rooted in nature, something that reflects clear and discrete variations in human identity. But race is also not an illusion. While it may not be 'real' in a biological sense, race is indeed real as a social category with definite social consequences."

Omi & Winant, Racial Formation in the United States (2015), quoted in McChesney (2015).

Sociologists answer in three steps. A category starts as an idea, turns into behavior, and ends up built into the system.

1 · Idea

People's beliefs

→ Stereotypes and prejudice: attitudes about what a racial group is like.

2 · Behavior

People's actions

→ Discrimination: treating someone unequally because of their group.

3 · System

Society's rules

→ Institutional discrimination: unequal treatment built into how hospitals, schools, employers and governments work, even when no one intends harm.

Idea → behavior → system. Put all three together and you have racism: a system of beliefs, practices and institutions that creates or reinforces unequal treatment based on race.

You've already seen this progression in the Quick Reads:

Quick Read #2 · idea → behavior

Who gets pain medicine

In U.S. emergency rooms, white patients with pain were more likely than Black or Hispanic patients to be prescribed opioids. A 2025 study of nearly 20,000 chronic-pain patients found Black and Hispanic patients were less likely to get opioids or referrals to pain specialists. Nothing about biology explains it. Doctors' assumptions do.

Sources: Pletcher et al. (2008), JAMA; Vargas et al. (2025), Journal of Pain Research.

Quick Read #4 · behavior

Who gets the callback

Researchers mailed nearly 5,000 fake résumés to real job ads in Boston and Chicago. Identical résumés with white-sounding names, like Emily or Greg, got about 50% more callbacks than ones with Black-sounding names, like Lakisha or Jamal. Same skills on paper, different result.

Source: Bertrand & Mullainathan (2004), "Are Emily and Greg More Employable than Lakisha and Jamal?" American Economic Review.

Part 2, again · system

Who gets counted

Census race data decides how voting districts are drawn, how civil rights laws protecting minority groups are enforced and where federal money goes. The boxes are invented, and the money is real.
